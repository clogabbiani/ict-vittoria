Unit 1 · Lesson 7

Remembering: memory and storage

In 1956 the first hard disk weighed nearly a tonne and held less than one photo from your phone.

Back to the film

In the film

In the film, decoded and secret messages pile up on desks and in boxes. We never see how anyone finds an old message again.

In reality

Bletchley Park kept huge card indexes of names, places, units and code words from earlier messages. The British Tabulating Machine Company supplied Hollerith punched-card machines to store and sort information: a kind of memory made of paper. Without that memory, a decoded message meant little.

01The story

Lesson 7 starts with the quiz on chapters 1 to 6: 14 questions in 15 minutes, on paper. Two questions are about the film. To revise, answer all the Check now questions of chapters 1 to 6 again.

In 1956 IBM announced the RAMAC, with the first hard disk. Its disk unit had 50 metal disks, each 61 cm across, and it weighed nearly a tonne. It held 5 million characters, about 3.75 MB in today's bytes: less than one phone photo. Nobody bought it: companies rented the whole system for about $3,200 a month. It was the first time a machine could go straight to any record, without reading all the others first.

In 1969 the Apollo 11 astronauts landed on the Moon with a small computer on board. Its programs were stored in core rope memory: a wire passing through a tiny ring meant 1, a wire going around it meant 0. Women at the Raytheon company wove this memory by hand. Many had worked in textile mills.

Engineers nicknamed it 'LOL memory', for 'little old ladies'. The nickname was unfair: the weaving was precise, expert work, and one wrong wire meant a wrong program. A funny name made a real skill invisible.

Margaret Hamilton led the team that wrote the Apollo software at MIT. During the Apollo 11 landing, the computer showed the alarms 1202 and 1201: it had too much work. The software was designed for this. It restarted, dropped the less important jobs and kept the most important ones, steering and landing, running. The landing went ahead.

02The science

RAM: the workbench

RAM (random access memory) holds what the computer is working on right now: open apps, the document you are typing. In the kitchen it is the workbench: the cook can only work on what is on the bench. RAM is very fast, but it forgets everything when the power goes off.

  • The cook = CPU core
  • The metronome = clock (GHz)
  • The countertop = cache
  • The workbench = RAM
  • The pantry = storage (SSD / HDD)

Storage: the pantry

Storage keeps your files, apps and photos, even with the power off. In the kitchen it is the pantry. Saving a file means copying it from the workbench (RAM) to the pantry (storage). That is why you lose your work if the power goes off before you save.

  • The cook = CPU core
  • The metronome = clock (GHz)
  • The countertop = cache
  • The workbench = RAM
  • The pantry = storage (SSD / HDD)

Two kinds of pantry: HDD and SSD

An HDD (hard disk drive) is the RAMAC's idea, made small: a metal plate spins and an arm moves over it to read and write. It is cheap and big, but slow. An SSD (solid-state drive) keeps data in flash chips. Nothing moves, so it is fast and silent, but it costs more.

  • The cook = CPU core
  • The metronome = clock (GHz)
  • The countertop = cache
  • The workbench = RAM
  • The pantry = storage (SSD / HDD)

How much, and how fast

A megabyte (MB) is about a million bytes. A gigabyte (GB) is about 1,000 MB, and a terabyte (TB) is about 1,000 GB. Speed is measured in MB/s: megabytes per second. If you copy 500 MB in 10 seconds, the speed is 500 ÷ 10 = 50 MB/s. A phone that is almost full gets slow, because it has no room to move data around.
