Unit 1 · Lesson 9

Can machines think? Today

In 1950 Turing asked: can machines think? Today you can type to a program that writes like a person. Now you know what is inside a computer. Has your answer changed?

01The story

In 1950 Alan Turing published a paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence". It begins with the question "Can machines think?". He proposed a game instead: a judge in another room types questions to two hidden players, a person and a machine. If the judge cannot tell which is which, should we say the machine thinks?

Today the machine in Turing's game has a name: a chatbot. A chatbot is a program that writes text by predicting the next word, again and again. It learned this from huge amounts of text: this is called training. Training and answering need millions of small sums at the same time, so chatbots run on GPUs, the chips built for 3D games in the 1990s.

Those GPUs sit in data centres: big buildings full of computers. Every answer uses electricity: in 2024 data centres used about 1.5% of all the world's electricity (IEA).

In 2025 researchers in California tested Turing's game. People chatted for five minutes, at the same time, with a real person and with a chatbot. They chose the chatbot (GPT-4.5) as "the human" 73% of the time. People often could not tell. That does not prove the chatbot thinks or understands.

In lesson 1 your class voted on Turing's question. Now you know about switches, the CPU, memory, the GPU and the OS. Vote again. Inside your computer today: the chatbot on your phone is an app; the hard work happens on GPUs in a data centre far away.

02The science

What a chatbot does

A chatbot continues text one word at a time. For each word it calculates which words are most likely to come next, using patterns it learned from huge amounts of writing. It does not look answers up in a book of true facts. All this is millions of small, similar sums: perfect work for a GPU, with its thousands of simple cooks working at the same time.

  • The cook = CPU core
  • The metronome = clock (GHz)
  • The countertop = cache
  • The workbench = RAM
  • The pantry = storage (SSD / HDD)

What a chatbot cannot do

A chatbot has no eyes, ears or hands: no senses. It remembers you only if the app saves your chats. It has no built-in way to know if it is right, so it can be wrong and still sound completely sure. So check what it says with a second source. Is predicting words the same as thinking? Turing left us the question; the AI unit in January goes further.

One photo, the whole unit (1)

Your friend takes a photo. The camera sensor turns light into numbers, using millions of tiny switches (the transistor, 1947). The CPU runs the camera program: fetch, decode, execute (the Intel 4004, 1971, the first CPU on one chip). While it works, the photo sits in RAM, the workbench (the Intel 1103 RAM chip, 1970). Then it is saved as a file in storage, the pantry (from punched cards in 1890 and the first hard disk in 1956 to the flash chips of today).

  • The cook = CPU core
  • The metronome = clock (GHz)
  • The countertop = cache
  • The workbench = RAM
  • The pantry = storage (SSD / HDD)

One photo, the whole unit (2)

The operating system gives the orders and gives the photo a name and a path. In the phone all these parts talk inside one chip, the SoC (the first iPhone, 2007); in a PC they talk across the motherboard (the IBM PC, 1981). A radio sends the bits through the air. On your friend's phone, the GPU turns the numbers back into millions of coloured pixels (the GPU, 1990s), and you tap the photo with your finger, not a typed command (mouse and windows, 1980s).

  • The cook = CPU core
  • The metronome = clock (GHz)
  • The countertop = cache
  • The workbench = RAM
  • The pantry = storage (SSD / HDD)

07In one sentence

Machines can now imitate us well; whether that is thinking is still Turing's question, and yours.
