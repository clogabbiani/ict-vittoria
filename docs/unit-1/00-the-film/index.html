Unit 1 · Lesson 0

The film: can machines think?

In 1950 Alan Turing asked a question that is still open: can machines think? Keep it in mind for the whole unit.

Back to the film

In the film

In the film, it is 1951 in Manchester: police come to Turing's house after a reported break-in, Turing sends them away, and a detective starts to suspect he is hiding something.

In reality

Turing's house near Manchester was burgled in January 1952, not 1951. When he reported it, he told the police about his relationship with a man, and he was charged. Detective Nock, who thinks Turing may be a spy, is an invented character.

01The story

We start the unit with a film: The Imitation Game (2014). The director is Morten Tyldum. Graham Moore wrote the screenplay, the text of the film, and he won an Oscar for it. The story comes from a biography, a book about a real life: Alan Turing: The Enigma, by Andrew Hodges (1983).

The film is about Bletchley Park, a country house in England. During the Second World War, thousands of people worked there in secret. Their job was codebreaking: reading enemy messages that were hidden with a code. The most famous code machine was the German Enigma.

Alan Turing was a mathematician. In 1936 he imagined, on paper, a machine that could do any calculation. At Bletchley Park he led Hut 8, the team that read the messages of the German navy. In 1950 he asked the question of our unit: can machines think?

Before you watch: the film is rated 12A in the UK and PG-13 in the US. It shows war, and it shows how Turing was persecuted, treated very unfairly by the law, because he was gay. He died in June 1954. His death was ruled a suicide; some historians question this. If something in the film upsets you, you can talk to your teacher or to the school counsellor.

Who's who? Many people in the film were real: Alan Turing, Joan Clarke, Hugh Alexander, Alastair Denniston, John Cairncross and Christopher Morcom, Turing's school friend. But the film changes what some of them did. Detective Nock, the policeman, is invented. A film is a dramatisation: a true story changed to make a better film.

02The science

Watch like a historian

A historian asks: what really happened, and how do we know? During the film, write short notes for six prompts: one machine you saw and what it did; one moment you think was invented; one person you want to know more about; what 'the imitation game' means; one question for the teacher; can machines think, and why?

What is a code?

A code, or cipher, hides a message with a rule and a key. The rule of the Caesar cipher is: move every letter forward in the alphabet. The key is how many places. With key 3, A becomes D and B becomes E. To read the message, you move the letters back by the same key.

A film is not a source

A source is where a fact comes from: a museum, an archive, a book by a historian. A film is a story. It can make us curious, but we check its facts in sources before we believe them. In the next lesson you will check the film's claims.

07In one sentence

A film can make us ask big questions, like 'Can machines think?', but we check its facts before we believe them.
