02The story

In 1950 Alan Turing wrote an article that begins with the question Can machines think? and then, in the very next sentence, says the question is useless: nobody agrees on what 'think' means. So he replaced it with a game. You chat by typing with two hidden players, a person and a machine. If you cannot tell which is which, Turing says, stop arguing about the word: the machine has earned it. That game is the imitation game, and the film took its title from it.

The machines in the film were real, but the film shows only one of them. The Bombe (1940) attacked Enigma, the cipher machine of the German army, navy and air force. It was electromechanical: spinning drums and electrical switches with moving parts, built for one job only: testing Enigma settings fast. It could not be told to do anything else. Three years later a telephone engineer, Tommy Flowers, built Colossus (1943) to attack a harder cipher used by Hitler's headquarters. Colossus was electronic: 1,500 glowing valves instead of moving parts, and it could be set up for different tasks. That is why Colossus, not the Bombe, is called the first programmable electronic computer.

Both machines were secret for thirty years. The Bombes were run by young women of the Women's Royal Naval Service on eight-hour shifts; Flowers paid for part of Colossus with his own money and was then ordered to burn the drawings. The first computers were born in a war and in silence.

Turing's bigger idea came earlier, in 1936, when he was 23. He imagined a machine made of a paper tape, a head that reads one square at a time, and a table of rules. Different tables, different machines. Then the master stroke: one special machine could read another machine's table from the tape and imitate it. One machine that can become any machine, just by being given instructions. That is a computer: the metal stays the same and the instructions change. It is why your phone can be a camera, a map and a chessboard in the same minute.

Nine years later the idea became metal. ENIAC (1945–46), in Philadelphia, was the first general-purpose electronic computer: about 18,000 valves, 27 tonnes, a room the size of a tennis court, as much electricity as a hundred kettles, and 5,000 additions per second. It was programmed by six women, by plugging cables and setting switches by hand. At the press day in 1946 they were not even introduced.

From there the story is the story of the switch getting smaller: the transistor (1947), the integrated circuit (1958), the first microprocessor, the Intel 4004 (1971), designed by Federico Faggin from Vicenza. Then the IBM PC (1981), the iPhone (2007) and ChatGPT (2022): Turing's game, played by a hundred million people.

03The notes

Everything from the lesson, in order. This is the part to study.

The Turing test (imitation game), 1950

In Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950) Turing proposed a test instead of a definition. An interrogator chats by text with two hidden players, a human and a machine, and must say which is which. If the machine is not identified reliably, it passes the test.

Why text? So that the voice, the face and the handwriting give nothing away: only the answers count.

The test is behavioural: it looks at what the machine says, not at what is inside it. That is exactly why this unit opens the box.

If you cannot tell the machine from the person, Turing says: stop arguing about the word 'think'.

The whole history of computing is the history of the switch: relay (mechanical) → valve (electronic) → transistor → integrated circuit. Same logic, smaller, faster, cheaper.

Turing's universal machine, 1936

An imaginary machine: an infinitely long tape of squares, a head that reads one square, writes a symbol and moves left or right, and a table of rules.

A universal machine can read any other machine's table from the tape and imitate it. One machine, any program: the idea of the general-purpose computer, nine years before one was built.

This is where hardware and software separate: the machine is fixed, the instructions on the tape change. The tape is memory; the table is the program.

Same metal, different instructions: that is what makes a computer a computer.

What is a computer system?

A computer is an electronic device that accepts input (data), processes it following a set of instructions (a program), stores data and instructions, and produces output (information).

  1. Input
  2. Process
  3. Output
  • Input: data goes in (keyboard, mouse, camera, sensor).
  • Process: the CPU follows the instructions of the program.
  • Output: information comes out (screen, printer, speaker, motor).
  • Storage: data and programs are kept for later (RAM while working, SSD or cloud for keeps).

Data is raw: 37.2. Information is data with a meaning: 'your temperature is 37.2 °C, slightly high'.

A computer system = hardware + software + data + people. Example: a washing machine. Input: the dial, a water sensor, a weight sensor. Process: the programme. Output: motor, pump, heater, beeper. Storage: the chosen settings and where it is in the cycle.

Hardware, software, firmware, data

What it isExamples
Hardwarethe physical parts: you can touch them (or drop them)CPU, RAM, motherboard, SSD, keyboard, monitor, printer, cables, router
Softwarethe programs: sets of instructions that tell the hardware what to do; you can copy them, not touch themWindows, Chrome, Minecraft, a Python script, a printer driver
Firmwaresoftware stored permanently on a chip inside a devicethe BIOS/UEFI of a PC, the program inside a printer or an Arduino
Datathe stuff the software works on; not hardware, not softwarea Word document, a photo, a Wi-Fi password

Analogy: the hardware is the piano, the software is the sheet music. Change the music and the same piano plays a different song.

Tricky cases: the Internet is a network (hardware + software + agreed rules, the protocols). A disc or cartridge is hardware; the game on it is software.

ENIAC, 1946: the facts

  • Built at the University of Pennsylvania (Mauchly and Eckert) to compute artillery tables; announced on 14 February 1946.
  • About 18,000 valves (vacuum tubes), 27 tonnes, 167 m² of floor, 150 kW of power (about 100 kettles), 5,000 additions per second.
  • No stored program: it was programmed by re-plugging cables and setting switches, which took days.
  • Programmed by six women: Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas, Ruth Lichterman. Not credited for decades; honoured in 1997.
  • Your phone is hundreds of millions of times faster, on a battery, in your pocket.
